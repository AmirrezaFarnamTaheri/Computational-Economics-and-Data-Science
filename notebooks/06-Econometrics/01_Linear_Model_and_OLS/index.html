    The Lens: The Cornerstone of Econometrics¶

    What problem are we solving? We want to understand the relationship between variables. Does education increase wages? Does investment increase growth? The Linear Regression Model is the starting point for answering these questions. It posits a linear relationship between a dependent variable \(y\) and independent variables \(X\), plus an error term.

    Why this method? Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is the "best" way to estimate this relationship under a set of assumptions (the Gauss-Markov theorem). It is the foundation upon which all of econometrics is built. Every advanced method (IV, GMM, Panel Data) is either an extension of OLS or a correction for when OLS fails.

    Economic question. In 01 Linear Model and OLS, what must remain economically invariant when the computational representation changes? The computational task only has economic meaning after the estimand and identifying assumptions are explicit. Ask what variation identifies the parameter, which observations act as the comparison group, and what data-generating process would make the estimator fail. A good empirical workflow pairs the point estimate with diagnostics, uncertainty, and at least one falsification or sensitivity check so that precision is not confused with identification.

    Learning Objectives¶

    • Derive the OLS estimator from first principles and state the Gauss-Markov assumptions.
    • Interpret regression coefficients, standard errors, t-statistics, and R-squared.
    • Diagnose violations (heteroskedasticity, multicollinearity, omitted variables) and apply corrections.
    • Implement OLS in Python using statsmodels and matrix algebra.

    Prerequisites¶

    • Linear Algebra: Matrix multiplication, inverses, and projection (Module 02).
    • Statistics: Mean, variance, hypothesis testing, and the Normal distribution.
    • Python: NumPy arrays, Pandas DataFrames, and basic Matplotlib.

    Historical Context — Legendre 1805, Frisch-Waugh 1933. Adrien-Marie Legendre published least squares in 1805; Carl Friedrich Gauss claimed he had used it since 1795, igniting statistics' first priority dispute. Ragnar Frisch and Frederick Waugh (1933, completed by Lovell 1963) proved partialling-out — the theorem hiding inside every fixed-effects and debiased regression.

    1. The Theory of Ordinary Least Squares¶

    Figure: OLS as the orthogonal projection of y onto col(X).. The linear model is \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\beta + \mathbf{u}\). The OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) minimizes the Sum of Squared Residuals (SSR) and is given by: $$ \hat{\beta}_{OLS} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} $$

    Dimension notes: \(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) (\(n\) observations); \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\) (\(k\) regressors, including the constant); \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^k\); the SSR maps \(\mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}\) and is minimized subject to \(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = k\), which makes \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) invertible.

    1.1 The Geometric Interpretation of OLS¶

    OLS has a powerful geometric interpretation. The vector of fitted values, \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\beta}\), is the orthogonal projection of the actual data vector \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the column space of X. The vector of residuals, \(\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}\), is the component of \(\mathbf{y}\) that is orthogonal to this space. The normal equations, \(\mathbf{X}'\hat{\mathbf{u}} = 0\), are the formal statement of this orthogonality.

    from IPython.display import Image

    1.2 Finite-Sample Properties (Gauss-Markov)¶

    Under the Classical Linear Model (CLM) Assumptions (including strict exogeneity and spherical errors, i.e., homoskedasticity and no serial correlation), the Gauss-Markov Theorem states that the OLS estimator is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE). It is the most efficient (minimum variance) estimator among all linear unbiased estimators.

    1.3 Asymptotic Properties¶

    Often, the strict CLM assumptions do not hold. However, OLS still has desirable asymptotic properties (properties in large samples) under weaker assumptions. - Consistency: If \(E[\mathbf{x}_i u_i] = 0\) (a weaker exogeneity assumption), then as the sample size \(n \to \infty\), the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) converges in probability to the true parameter \(\beta\). This relies on the Law of Large Numbers. - Asymptotic Normality: Under the same weak exogeneity assumption and finite fourth moments of the errors, the distribution of the OLS estimator approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows. This relies on the Central Limit Theorem and justifies the use of t-tests and F-tests in large samples, even if the errors are not normally distributed.

    2. The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell Theorem¶

    The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem provides a deep understanding of what a multiple regression coefficient represents. It states that the coefficient \(\beta_1\) in a regression of \(y\) on \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) can be obtained in three steps: 1. Regress \(y\) on \(X_2\) and obtain the residuals, \(\tilde{y}\). 2. Regress \(X_1\) on \(X_2\) and obtain the residuals, \(\tilde{X_1}\). 3. Regress \(\tilde{y}\) on \(\tilde{X_1}\). The coefficient from this simple regression is identical to \(\beta_1\) from the original multiple regression.

    This process, sometimes called "partialling out", has profound implications. It shows that the coefficient \(\beta_1\) captures the effect of \(X_1\) on \(y\) after accounting for the effect of \(X_2\). In essence, \(\beta_1\) is the effect of the part of \(X_1\) that is orthogonal to (i.e., uncorrelated with) \(X_2\). This is the mathematical foundation for interpreting regression coefficients as capturing the effect of one variable, "holding all other variables constant."

    # --- Numerical Proof of Frisch-Waugh-Lovell Theorem ---

# Generate data
n = 100
x2 = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
x3 = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
y = 1 + 2*x2 + 3*x3 + rng.normal(0, 1, n)

# 1. Full Regression
X = pd.DataFrame({'const': 1, 'x2': x2, 'x3': x3})
model_full = sm.OLS(y, X).fit()
beta_x2_full = model_full.params['x2']

# 2. Partialling out x3
# Regress y on x3 (and const)
res_y = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x3)).fit().resid
# Regress x2 on x3 (and const)
res_x2 = sm.OLS(x2, sm.add_constant(x3)).fit().resid

# 3. Regress residuals on residuals
# Note: No constant needed here as residuals have mean zero
model_fwl = sm.OLS(res_y, res_x2).fit()
beta_x2_fwl = model_fwl.params[0]

print(f"Full Regression Coefficient for x2: {beta_x2_full:.6f}")
print(f"FWL (Partialled) Coefficient for x2: {beta_x2_fwl:.6f}")
print(f"Difference: {abs(beta_x2_full - beta_x2_fwl):.10e}")
print("> **Note:** The coefficients are identical (within floating point precision), numerically proving the FWL theorem.")

    3. Model Diagnostics¶

    3.1 Heteroskedasticity¶

    Heteroskedasticity occurs when the variance of the error term is not constant (\(Var(u_i) = \sigma_i^2\)). OLS remains unbiased, but its standard errors are incorrect. We can test for it using the Breusch-Pagan test and correct the standard errors using heteroskedasticity-robust estimators.

    # --- Testing for Heteroskedasticity ---
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import het_breuschpagan

# Generate heteroskedastic data
X_het = pd.DataFrame({'const': 1, 'x': rng.uniform(0, 10, 100)})
u = rng.normal(0, 1 + 0.5 * X_het['x'], 100) # Variance increases with x
y_het = 1 + 2 * X_het['x'] + u

model_het = sm.OLS(y_het, X_het).fit()

# Breusch-Pagan Test
# H0: Homoskedasticity
lm, p_lm, fval, p_fval = het_breuschpagan(model_het.resid, model_het.model.exog)

print("Breusch-Pagan Test Results:")
print(f"LM Statistic: {lm:.4f}")
print(f"P-Value: {p_lm:.4f}")

if p_lm < 0.05:
    print("> **Note:** We reject the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity. Evidence of heteroskedasticity found.")
else:
    print("> **Note:** We fail to reject the null hypothesis.")

# Plotting residuals vs fitted
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.scatter(model_het.fittedvalues, model_het.resid)
plt.xlabel("Fitted Values")
plt.ylabel("Residuals")
plt.axhline(0, color='red', linestyle='--')
plt.title("Residuals vs Fitted Values")
plt.show()

    3.2 Multicollinearity¶

    Multicollinearity occurs when regressors are highly correlated. It does not bias the coefficients, but it inflates their variance, making estimates imprecise. We can detect it using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A VIF greater than 5 or 10 is a common red flag.

    # --- Checking for Multicollinearity (VIF) ---

# Generate collinear data
x1 = rng.normal(0, 1, 100)
x2 = 2 * x1 + rng.normal(0, 0.1, 100) # Highly correlated with x1
X_col = pd.DataFrame({'const': 1, 'x1': x1, 'x2': x2})

vif_data = pd.DataFrame()
vif_data["feature"] = X_col.columns
vif_data["VIF"] = [variance_inflation_factor(X_col.values, i) for i in range(len(X_col.columns))]

display(vif_data)
print("> **Note:** A VIF > 5 or 10 indicates high multicollinearity. Here, x1 and x2 have high VIFs because they are correlated.")

    4. Advanced Topics¶

    4.1 Generalized Least Squares (GLS)¶

    When the error covariance matrix is not spherical (\(E[\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}'|\mathbf{X}] \ne \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}\)), OLS is no longer BLUE. The efficient estimator is Generalized Least Squares (GLS): $$ \hat{\beta}_{GLS} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{\Omega}{-1}\mathbf{X}) $$ In practice, the true error covariance matrix } \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{y\(\mathbf{\Omega}\) is unknown and must be estimated. This leads to Feasible GLS (FGLS), a two-step procedure where we use the residuals from a first-stage OLS regression to estimate \(\mathbf{\Omega}\).

    # --- Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) ---

# Using the heteroskedastic data from earlier (y_het, X_het)
# Step 1: OLS (Already done: model_het)
residuals = model_het.resid

# Step 2: Estimate variance function
# Assume log(sigma^2) = alpha0 + alpha1 * x
log_u2 = np.log(residuals**2)
model_var = sm.OLS(log_u2, X_het).fit()
predicted_var = np.exp(model_var.fittedvalues)

# Step 3: WLS
# Weights are 1/variance
weights = 1.0 / predicted_var
model_fgls = sm.WLS(y_het, X_het, weights=weights).fit()

print("OLS Standard Errors:")
print(model_het.bse)
print("\nFGLS Standard Errors:")
print(model_fgls.bse)
print("\n> **Note:** FGLS standard errors are generally smaller (more efficient) when heteroskedasticity is correctly modeled.")

    4.2 Regularization for High-Dimensional Models¶

    In high-dimensional settings (where the number of predictors \(p\) is close to or larger than the number of observations \(n\)), OLS performs poorly or fails completely. Regularization methods solve this by adding a penalty term to the minimization problem, which introduces some bias to dramatically reduce variance. - Ridge Regression (L2 Penalty): Minimizes \(SSR + \lambda \sum \beta_j^2\). It shrinks coefficients towards zero. - LASSO (L1 Penalty): Minimizes \(SSR + \lambda \sum |\beta_j|\). The L1 penalty has the crucial property that it can shrink some coefficients to be exactly zero, thus performing automatic variable selection.

    ### LASSO for Variable Selection in a High-Dimensional Setting
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N, P_total, P_true = 100, 200, 10 # 100 obs, 200 potential predictors, only 10 are non-zero

X = rng.standard_normal((N, P_total))
true_beta = np.zeros(P_total); true_beta[:P_true] = rng.uniform(-2, 2, P_true)
y = X @ true_beta + rng.normal(0, 2, size=N)

# Use LassoCV to find the best penalty parameter alpha via cross-validation
lasso_cv = LassoCV(cv=10, random_state=42).fit(X, y)

print(f"> **Note:** LASSO with cross-validation selected alpha = {lasso_cv.alpha_:.4f}")
estimated_beta = lasso_cv.coef_

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 7))
ax.stem(np.arange(P_total), true_beta, linefmt='k-', markerfmt='ko', basefmt='k-', label='True Coefficients')
ax.stem(np.arange(P_total), estimated_beta, linefmt='r--', markerfmt='rx', basefmt='r--', label='LASSO Estimates')
ax.set_title('LASSO for Variable Selection'); ax.set_xlabel('Coefficient Index'); ax.legend()
plt.show()
print("> **Note:** LASSO correctly identifies most of the true non-zero coefficients and shrinks the vast majority of the irrelevant coefficients to exactly zero. OLS would be unable to run in this p > n setting.")

    4.3 Bayesian Linear Regression with Gibbs Sampling¶

    The Bayesian approach treats the parameters \(\beta\) and \(\sigma^2\) as random variables. We place priors on them and use the data to update these to a posterior distribution. For the linear model with standard conjugate priors, we can use a highly efficient MCMC method called a Gibbs Sampler.

    A Gibbs sampler works by iteratively sampling from the full conditional posterior distribution of each parameter, holding the others fixed. For the linear model, these distributions have known analytical forms: - \(p(\beta | y, X, \sigma^2) \sim \text{Normal}(\dots)\) - \(p(\sigma^2 | y, X, \beta) \sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\dots)\)

    # --- Bayesian Linear Regression via Gibbs Sampling ---

class BayesianLinearRegression:
    def __init__(self, n_iter=2000, burn_in=500):
        self.n_iter = n_iter
        self.burn_in = burn_in
        self.beta_samples = []
        self.sigma2_samples = []

    def fit(self, X, y):
        n, k = X.shape
        # Priors (Non-informative / Conjugate)
        # Beta ~ N(mu0, Sigma0)
        mu0 = np.zeros(k)
        Sigma0 = np.eye(k) * 100
        Sigma0_inv = np.linalg.inv(Sigma0)

        # Sigma2 ~ IG(alpha0, beta0)
        alpha0 = 2.0
        beta0 = 1.0

        # Initialization
        beta = np.zeros(k)
        sigma2 = 1.0

        # Precompute X'X and X'y
        XtX = X.T @ X
        Xty = X.T @ y

        for i in range(self.n_iter):
            # 1. Update Beta | y, X, sigma2
            # Posterior covariance: V_n = (Sigma0^-1 + sigma2^-1 * X'X)^-1
            V_n_inv = Sigma0_inv + (1/sigma2) * XtX
            V_n = np.linalg.inv(V_n_inv)
            # Posterior mean: mu_n = V_n * (Sigma0^-1 * mu0 + sigma2^-1 * X'y)
            mu_n = V_n @ (Sigma0_inv @ mu0 + (1/sigma2) * Xty)

            beta = np.random.multivariate_normal(mu_n, V_n)

            # 2. Update Sigma2 | y, X, beta
            # Posterior alpha: alpha_n = alpha0 + n/2
            alpha_n = alpha0 + n / 2
            # Posterior beta: beta_n = beta0 + 0.5 * (y - X*beta)'(y - X*beta)
            resid = y - X @ beta
            beta_n = beta0 + 0.5 * (resid.T @ resid)

            # Numpy's gamma is shape, scale (where scale = 1/beta)
            sigma2 = 1.0 / np.random.gamma(alpha_n, 1.0/beta_n)

            if i >= self.burn_in:
                self.beta_samples.append(beta)
                self.sigma2_samples.append(sigma2)

        self.beta_samples = np.array(self.beta_samples)
        self.sigma2_samples = np.array(self.sigma2_samples)

    def plot_posteriors(self, param_names):
        k = self.beta_samples.shape[1]
        fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, k+1, figsize=(15, 4))

        for i in range(k):
            sns.histplot(self.beta_samples[:, i], kde=True, ax=axes[i])
            axes[i].set_title(f'Posterior of {param_names[i]}')

        sns.histplot(self.sigma2_samples, kde=True, ax=axes[k])
        axes[k].set_title('Posterior of Sigma^2')
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.show()

# Run the sampler
print("Running Gibbs Sampler...")
# Use simple data for clarity
n_bayes, k_bayes = 100, 3
X_bayes = rng.normal(0, 1, (n_bayes, k_bayes))
X_bayes = np.hstack([np.ones((n_bayes, 1)), X_bayes]) # Add constant
true_beta_bayes = np.array([1.0, 2.0, -1.0, 0.5])
y_bayes = X_bayes @ true_beta_bayes + rng.normal(0, 1, n_bayes)

bayes_model = BayesianLinearRegression(n_iter=5000, burn_in=1000)
bayes_model.fit(X_bayes, y_bayes)
bayes_model.plot_posteriors(['Intercept', 'Beta1', 'Beta2', 'Beta3'])

print(f"True Betas: {true_beta_bayes}")
print(f"Estimated Betas (Posterior Mean): {bayes_model.beta_samples.mean(axis=0)}")

    Common Pitfalls in This Lecture

    • Omitted variable bias. Leaving out a variable correlated with both \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) biases every remaining coefficient — direction given by the classic covariance formula. Conversely, controlling for colliders/post-treatment variables creates bias where none existed. Draw the causal graph first.
    • Multicollinearity panic. High pairwise correlations inflate variances (high VIF) but do not bias coefficients. Mechanically dropping 'redundant' controls can induce omitted-variable bias; address multicollinearity only when it blocks inference you actually need.

    Key Equations¶

    • Linear model: \(y=X\beta+u\).
    • OLS estimator: \(\hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'y\) when \(X\) has full column rank.
    • Projection: \(\hat y=P_Xy\) with \(P_X=X(X'X)^{-1}X'\).
    • FWL residualization: the coefficient on a regressor block can be obtained by regressing residualized \(y\) on residualized regressors.
    • HC covariance: sandwich estimators replace homoskedastic \(\sigma^2I\) with residual-based diagonal weighting.

    Summary¶

    OLS is the hammer of the econometrician's toolkit.

    Key Takeaways: * Projection: OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals. Geometrically, the residual vector is orthogonal to the regressors. * Frisch-Waugh-Lovell: A crucial theorem that allows us to understand multivariate regression as a sequence of univariate regressions (partialling out). * Assumptions Matter: OLS is only unbiased and consistent if the error term is uncorrelated with the regressors (Strict Exogeneity).

    3. LASSO vs. Ridge: In the high-dimensional example, replace the LassoCV with RidgeCV. How do the estimated coefficients differ? Why does Ridge not perform variable selection?

    4. Gibbs Sampler: Implement a Gibbs sampler for the Bayesian linear model from scratch. You will need to code the loop that iteratively draws from the conditional posterior for \(\beta\) (a multivariate normal) and \(\sigma^2\) (an inverse-gamma). Compare the posterior means from your sampler to the results from the MCMCSampler class.

    5. Horseshoe Prior: The Horseshoe prior is a popular choice for sparse Bayesian models. Research its properties. How does it differ from a standard Laplace (Bayesian LASSO) prior? Why is it often considered to have better theoretical properties?
