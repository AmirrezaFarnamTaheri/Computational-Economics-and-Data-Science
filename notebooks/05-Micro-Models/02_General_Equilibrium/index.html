    The Lens: The Invisible Hand Calculated¶

    What problem are we solving? Partial equilibrium analyzes one market in isolation (e.g., the market for apples). General Equilibrium (GE) analyzes all markets simultaneously. It asks: Can prices adjust so that every market clears at the same time? This framework captures the ripple effects of shocks: how a tax on steel affects the price of cars, the wages of auto workers, and the demand for housing in Detroit.

    Why this method? We solve for a vector of prices such that excess demand is zero in all markets. * Exchange Economy: Pure trade between agents with endowments (Edgeworth Box). * Production Economy: Firms transform inputs into outputs (Robinson Crusoe). * Heckscher-Ohlin: A classic GE model of international trade. This notebook implements the computational search for the price vector that balances the world.

    Economic question. In 02 General Equilibrium, what must remain economically invariant when the computational representation changes? Microeconomic computation makes equilibrium and incentive constraints operational. The useful question is how preferences, technologies, information, or strategic beliefs map into choices and welfare, and whether the computed solution respects feasibility, optimality, and equilibrium conditions. Whenever multiple equilibria or corner solutions are possible, the numerical method should expose rather than hide that economic structure.

    • Implement the Heckscher-Ohlin trade model (2 countries, 2 goods, 2 factors).

    1. Theoretical Foundations: Exchange, Efficiency, and Existence¶

    Before computing equilibria numerically, we establish what we are computing and why it is worth computing. Three questions organize the classical theory, and each has a precise answer:

    1. What is an equilibrium allocation, and what makes it "efficient"? → the Edgeworth box and the contract curve.
    2. Is the market outcome socially good? → the First and Second Welfare Theorems.
    3. Does an equilibrium even exist? → Brouwer's fixed-point theorem.

    The third question is not pedantic: the numerical solvers used later in this notebook search for a price vector that clears all markets. If no such vector existed, the solver would be chasing a ghost, and its failure to converge would tell us nothing about our code.

    1.1 The Edgeworth Box and the Contract Curve¶

    Consider the simplest possible general-equilibrium economy: two agents (\(A\) and \(B\)), two goods (\(x\) and \(y\)), no production, and fixed total endowments \(\bar{x}, \bar{y}\). Because every unit not consumed by \(A\) must be consumed by \(B\), an allocation is fully described by \(A\)'s bundle alone: \((x_B, y_B) = (\bar{x} - x_A,\; \bar{y} - y_A)\).

    This is what makes the Edgeworth box possible: a rectangle of width \(\bar{x}\) and height \(\bar{y}\) in which every point is a complete allocation of the whole economy. Agent \(A\)'s consumption is measured from the bottom-left origin, agent \(B\)'s from the top-right origin — reading \(B\)'s axes upside-down. \(A\)'s indifference curves bow outward from the lower-left; \(B\)'s bow outward from the upper-right.

    Definition (Pareto efficiency). An allocation is Pareto efficient if no reallocation can make one agent better off without making the other worse off.

    Geometrically, an allocation is Pareto efficient exactly when the two agents' indifference curves are tangent. If they merely cross, they carve out a lens-shaped region of allocations that both agents strictly prefer — so a mutually beneficial trade is available and the allocation was not efficient. Tangency is equivalent to equality of the marginal rates of substitution:

    \[ MRS_A \;=\; \frac{\partial u_A/\partial x}{\partial u_A/\partial y} \;=\; \frac{\partial u_B/\partial x}{\partial u_B/\partial y} \;=\; MRS_B. \]

    Definition (Contract curve). The contract curve is the locus of all Pareto-efficient allocations in the box — the set of tangency points, running from \(A\)'s origin to \(B\)'s origin.

    Worked example (used in the code below). With Cobb-Douglas preferences \(u_A = x^{\alpha_A} y^{1-\alpha_A}\) and \(u_B = x^{\alpha_B} y^{1-\alpha_B}\), the marginal rate of substitution has the closed form

    \[ MRS = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{y}{x}. \]

    Setting \(MRS_A = MRS_B\) with \(k_i \equiv \alpha_i/(1-\alpha_i)\) and substituting \(B\)'s consumption:

    \[ k_A \frac{y_A}{x_A} = k_B \frac{\bar{y} - y_A}{\bar{x} - x_A} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; k_A\, y_A (\bar{x} - x_A) = k_B\, (\bar{y} - y_A)\, x_A. \]

    Collecting the \(y_A\) terms gives the contract curve in closed form:

    \[ y_A(x_A) \;=\; \frac{k_B\, \bar{y}\, x_A}{k_A(\bar{x} - x_A) + k_B\, x_A}. \]

    This is exactly the contract_curve_y function in the code cell below — the curve is not sketched by hand, it is computed from the tangency condition.

    1.2 Walrasian Equilibrium¶

    A Walrasian (competitive) equilibrium is a price vector \(p^* = (p_x, p_y)\) and an allocation such that (i) each agent demands the bundle maximizing utility subject to their budget constraint, evaluated at their endowment's market value, and (ii) all markets clear.

    Only relative prices matter: scaling all prices by \(\lambda > 0\) scales every budget constraint by \(\lambda\) and leaves demands unchanged (demand functions are homogeneous of degree zero in prices). We therefore normalize by choosing a numeraire — below, \(p_y \equiv 1\).

    Walras' Law. At any price vector, the value of aggregate excess demand is identically zero: \(p \cdot z(p) = 0\), where \(z(p)\) is the vector of excess demands.

    Proof. Each agent's budget constraint binds when preferences are locally non-satiated: \(p \cdot (\text{demand}_i) = p \cdot (\text{endowment}_i)\), i.e. the value of each agent's excess demand is zero. Summing over agents gives \(p \cdot z(p) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

    The practical payoff: with \(N\) markets, if \(N-1\) clear then the \(N\)-th clears automatically (since \(p\cdot z = 0\) and the first \(N-1\) components of \(z\) vanish, the \(N\)-th must too, provided \(p_N \neq 0\)). This is why the solvers below search over \(N-1\) prices, not \(N\) — the system would otherwise be redundant and the Jacobian singular.

    1.3 The Two Fundamental Welfare Theorems¶

    First Fundamental Welfare Theorem. If preferences are locally non-satiated, then every Walrasian equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient.

    Proof (by contradiction). Suppose the equilibrium allocation \(\{c_i^*\}\) at prices \(p^*\) is not efficient: there is a feasible allocation \(\{\hat{c}_i\}\) that every agent weakly prefers and at least one strictly prefers. For the agent who strictly prefers \(\hat{c}_i\): since \(c_i^*\) was chosen as the best affordable bundle, \(\hat{c}_i\) must have been unaffordable, so \(p^* \cdot \hat{c}_i > p^* \cdot e_i\) (where \(e_i\) is the endowment). For any agent who is merely weakly better off, local non-satiation gives \(p^* \cdot \hat{c}_i \ge p^* \cdot e_i\) — if \(\hat{c}_i\) were strictly cheaper, some bundle arbitrarily near \(\hat{c}_i\) would be both affordable and strictly better than \(c_i^*\), contradicting optimality of \(c_i^*\). Summing over all agents, the strict inequality survives:

    \[ p^* \cdot \sum_i \hat{c}_i \;>\; p^* \cdot \sum_i e_i. \]

    But \(\{\hat{c}_i\}\) was assumed feasible, meaning \(\sum_i \hat{c}_i = \sum_i e_i\), which forces the two sides to be equal — a contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

    Notice how little the theorem assumes: no convexity, no differentiability, only local non-satiation. Notice also what it does not say — nothing about fairness. An equilibrium in which one agent owns everything is Pareto efficient.

    Second Fundamental Welfare Theorem. If preferences are additionally convex (and continuous, non-satiated), then any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a Walrasian equilibrium after a suitable lump-sum redistribution of endowments.

    Proof sketch. Fix a Pareto-efficient allocation \(\{c_i^*\}\). For each agent, form the set of bundles strictly preferred to \(c_i^*\); convexity of preferences makes each such set convex, hence so is their aggregate sum \(V\). Efficiency means \(V\) does not intersect the set of feasible aggregate bundles. Two disjoint convex sets can be separated: the separating hyperplane theorem (proved in Appendix/A1-Real-Analysis.ipynb) supplies a nonzero price vector \(p^*\) such that everything in \(V\) costs at least as much as the aggregate endowment. That \(p^*\) is precisely a supporting price system: give each agent an endowment worth \(p^* \cdot c_i^*\), and \(c_i^*\) becomes their utility-maximizing choice. \(\blacksquare\)

    Why convexity is indispensable here (but not for the First Theorem). With a non-convex preferred set, no hyperplane can separate it from the feasible set without cutting through — the tangency price would tempt the agent to jump to a distant bundle on the far side of the "dent" rather than stay put. Economically, the Second Theorem is the formal statement that efficiency and distribution are separable concerns: society can choose any efficient point it considers fair and decentralize it through markets, provided redistribution is lump-sum. The caveat is severe in practice, since real-world taxes distort behavior at the margin and are therefore not lump-sum.

    1.4 Existence: Brouwer's Fixed-Point Theorem¶

    Nothing so far guarantees an equilibrium price vector exists. This is the deepest result of the three, and the argument is beautiful: turn "market clearing" into "having a fixed point."

    Brouwer's Fixed-Point Theorem. Every continuous function \(f : K \to K\) mapping a non-empty, compact, convex set \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) into itself has at least one fixed point: some \(p\) with \(f(p) = p\).

    Applying it to markets. Because demands are homogeneous of degree zero, we can restrict prices to the unit simplex \(\Delta = \{ p \in \mathbb{R}^n_{+} : \sum_j p_j = 1 \}\), which is non-empty, compact, and convex — exactly Brouwer's hypotheses. Now define the price-adjustment map (Gale-Nikaido), which raises the relative price of any good in excess demand:

    \[ f_j(p) \;=\; \frac{p_j + \max\{0,\, z_j(p)\}}{1 + \sum_{k} \max\{0,\, z_k(p)\}}, \qquad j = 1, \ldots, n. \]

    The denominator renormalizes so that \(f(p)\) stays in \(\Delta\). If the excess demand function \(z\) is continuous, so is \(f\), and Brouwer applies: a fixed point \(p^*\) exists.

    Why a fixed point is an equilibrium. At a fixed point, \(p_j^* = f_j(p^*)\) for every \(j\). Write \(\mu = \sum_k \max\{0, z_k(p^*)\} \ge 0\); the fixed-point condition rearranges to \(p_j^*\, \mu = \max\{0,\, z_j(p^*)\}\) for all \(j\). Multiply by \(z_j(p^*)\) and sum over \(j\):

    \[ \mu \underbrace{\sum_j p_j^* z_j(p^*)}_{= \,0 \text{ by Walras' Law}} \;=\; \sum_j z_j(p^*) \max\{0,\, z_j(p^*)\} \;=\; \sum_j \big[\max\{0,\, z_j(p^*)\}\big]^2 . \]

    The left side is zero by Walras' Law. The right side is a sum of squares, so every term must vanish: \(\max\{0, z_j(p^*)\} = 0\) for all \(j\), i.e. \(z_j(p^*) \le 0\) for every good — no good is in excess demand. Combined with Walras' Law and positive prices, this gives \(z_j(p^*) = 0\): all markets clear. \(\blacksquare\)

    What the existence proof demands, and what it costs us. Continuity of \(z\) requires preferences to be continuous and convex (with non-convexities, demand jumps discontinuously as a price crosses a threshold and the fixed point can evaporate). Note also what the theorem does not deliver: uniqueness. Multiple equilibria are entirely possible, which is why the numerical solvers below can converge to different answers from different starting guesses — and why reporting the starting point matters when publishing CGE results.

    With existence, efficiency, and decentralization settled, we can turn to actually computing equilibria.

    # --- The Edgeworth Box: Equilibrium and the Contract Curve ---
# A two-agent, two-good pure exchange economy with Cobb-Douglas preferences,
# u_i(x, y) = x^alpha_i * y^(1 - alpha_i). Good Y is the numeraire (p_y = 1).

TOTAL_X, TOTAL_Y = 10.0, 10.0          # Aggregate endowments (box dimensions)
ALPHA_A, ALPHA_B = 0.6, 0.3            # Cobb-Douglas expenditure share on good X
ENDOW_A = np.array([8.0, 2.0])         # Agent A starts X-rich, Y-poor
ENDOW_B = np.array([TOTAL_X, TOTAL_Y]) - ENDOW_A


def utility(bundle: np.ndarray, alpha: float) -> float:
    """Cobb-Douglas utility u = x^alpha * y^(1-alpha)."""
    x, y = bundle
    return x**alpha * y ** (1 - alpha)


def demand(alpha: float, endowment: np.ndarray, prices: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Cobb-Douglas demand: spend fraction alpha of wealth on X, the rest on Y."""
    wealth = prices @ endowment
    return np.array([alpha * wealth / prices[0], (1 - alpha) * wealth / prices[1]])


def excess_demand_x(price_x: float) -> float:
    """Aggregate excess demand for good X. By Walras' Law, zeroing this
    clears the Y market automatically -- so we solve ONE equation, not two."""
    prices = np.array([price_x, 1.0])
    total_demand = (demand(ALPHA_A, ENDOW_A, prices)[0]
                    + demand(ALPHA_B, ENDOW_B, prices)[0])
    return total_demand - TOTAL_X


def contract_curve_y(x_a: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Pareto-efficient y_A as a function of x_A, from MRS_A = MRS_B.
    Derived in section 1.1: y_A = k_B * Ybar * x_A / (k_A(Xbar - x_A) + k_B x_A)."""
    k_a = ALPHA_A / (1 - ALPHA_A)
    k_b = ALPHA_B / (1 - ALPHA_B)
    return k_b * TOTAL_Y * x_a / (k_a * (TOTAL_X - x_a) + k_b * x_a)


# --- Solve for the Walrasian equilibrium price ---
# Closed form: setting excess demand to zero and solving the linear equation in p_x.
price_x_analytic = (ALPHA_A * ENDOW_A[1] + ALPHA_B * ENDOW_B[1]) / (
    TOTAL_X - ALPHA_A * ENDOW_A[0] - ALPHA_B * ENDOW_B[0]
)
price_x_star = brentq(excess_demand_x, 1e-6, 1e3)   # numerical confirmation
assert np.isclose(price_x_star, price_x_analytic), "closed form must match the solver"

prices_star = np.array([price_x_star, 1.0])
alloc_a = demand(ALPHA_A, ENDOW_A, prices_star)
alloc_b = demand(ALPHA_B, ENDOW_B, prices_star)

# --- Verify the theory numerically ---
# 1. Both markets clear (Walras' Law: we only imposed the X market).
assert np.isclose(alloc_a[0] + alloc_b[0], TOTAL_X), "X market must clear"
assert np.isclose(alloc_a[1] + alloc_b[1], TOTAL_Y), "Y market clears by Walras' Law"
# 2. First Welfare Theorem: the equilibrium allocation must be Pareto efficient,
#    i.e. it must lie exactly on the contract curve.
assert np.isclose(contract_curve_y(alloc_a[0]), alloc_a[1]), \
    "equilibrium must be Pareto efficient (First Welfare Theorem)"

# --- Plot the Edgeworth box ---
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 8))

x_grid = np.linspace(0.05, TOTAL_X - 0.05, 400)

# Indifference curves through the equilibrium allocation, for both agents.
u_a_star, u_b_star = utility(alloc_a, ALPHA_A), utility(alloc_b, ALPHA_B)
# For A: u = x^a y^(1-a)  =>  y = (u / x^a)^(1/(1-a))
y_a_star = (u_a_star / x_grid**ALPHA_A) ** (1 / (1 - ALPHA_A))
# For B, measured from the top-right origin: x_B = Xbar - x_A, y_B = Ybar - y_A
x_b_grid = TOTAL_X - x_grid
y_b_star = TOTAL_Y - (u_b_star / x_b_grid**ALPHA_B) ** (1 / (1 - ALPHA_B))

ax.plot(x_grid, y_a_star, color='tab:blue', lw=2,
        label="A's indifference curve through equilibrium")
ax.plot(x_grid, y_b_star, color='tab:red', lw=2,
        label="B's indifference curve through equilibrium")
ax.plot(x_grid, contract_curve_y(x_grid), color='black', lw=2.5, ls='--',
        label='Contract curve (Pareto set)')

# Budget line through the endowment with slope -p_x / p_y.
budget_y = ENDOW_A[1] + price_x_star * (ENDOW_A[0] - x_grid)
inside = (budget_y >= 0) & (budget_y <= TOTAL_Y)
ax.plot(x_grid[inside], budget_y[inside], color='tab:green', lw=1.8, alpha=0.9,
        label=f'Budget line (slope $-p_x/p_y = -{price_x_star:.3f}$)')

ax.plot(*ENDOW_A, 'o', color='tab:orange', ms=12, zorder=5,
        label=f'Endowment  ({ENDOW_A[0]:.1f}, {ENDOW_A[1]:.1f})')
ax.plot(*alloc_a, '*', color='black', ms=20, zorder=5,
        label=f'Equilibrium  ({alloc_a[0]:.2f}, {alloc_a[1]:.2f})')

ax.set_xlim(0, TOTAL_X)
ax.set_ylim(0, TOTAL_Y)
ax.set_xlabel("Good X  →  (Agent A)")
ax.set_ylabel("Good Y  →  (Agent A)")
ax.set_title('The Edgeworth Box: Trade from Endowment to Equilibrium',
             fontsize=14, fontweight='bold')

# Mirrored axes so agent B's consumption can be read from the top-right origin.
ax_b = ax.secondary_xaxis('top', functions=(lambda v: TOTAL_X - v, lambda v: TOTAL_X - v))
ax_b.set_xlabel("(Agent B)  ←  Good X")
ay_b = ax.secondary_yaxis('right', functions=(lambda v: TOTAL_Y - v, lambda v: TOTAL_Y - v))
ay_b.set_ylabel("(Agent B)  ←  Good Y")

ax.legend(loc='lower left', fontsize=9, framealpha=0.95)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

display(Markdown(
    f"> **Equilibrium price** $p_x/p_y = {price_x_star:.4f}$. "
    f"Agent A trades from the endowment $({ENDOW_A[0]:.1f}, {ENDOW_A[1]:.1f})$ to "
    f"$({alloc_a[0]:.2f}, {alloc_a[1]:.2f})$, selling X to buy Y.\n\n"
    f"> The equilibrium star sits **exactly on the contract curve** (asserted above), "
    f"and the two indifference curves are **tangent** there — the First Welfare "
    f"Theorem, verified numerically rather than merely asserted."
))

    2. The Computable General Equilibrium (CGE) Framework¶

    We consider a closed economy with: - Two Factors: Labor (\(L\)) and Capital (\(K\)). - Two Sectors: Manufacturing (\(X\)) and Agriculture (\(Y\)). - Households: Own the factors and consume the goods.

    2.1 The Model Equations¶

    An equilibrium is a vector of prices \((P_X, P_Y, W, R)\) such that:

    1. Zero Profit Conditions: Price equals unit cost. $$ P_X = c_X(W, R), \quad P_Y = c_Y(W, R) $$ assuming Cobb-Douglas technologies: \(Y_i = A_i K_i^{\alpha_i} L_i^{1-\alpha_i}\).

    2. Market Clearing (Goods): Supply equals Demand. $$ Y_X = D_X(P_X, P_Y, I), \quad Y_Y = D_Y(P_X, P_Y, I) $$ where \(I = W L_{bar} + R K_{bar}\) is total income.

    3. Market Clearing (Factors): $$ L_X + L_Y = L_{bar}, \quad K_X + K_Y = K_{bar} $$

    By Walras' Law, if \(N-1\) markets clear, the \(N^{th}\) market clears automatically. We normalize one price (numeraire), usually \(W=1\).

    class CGEModel:
    """
    A 2-Sector (X, Y), 2-Factor (L, K) General Equilibrium Model.
    Technology: Cobb-Douglas.
    Preferences: Cobb-Douglas.
    """
    def __init__(self, L_bar=100, K_bar=50, alpha_X=0.3, alpha_Y=0.7, beta_c=0.5, A_X=1.0, A_Y=1.0):
        # Endowments
        self.L_bar = L_bar
        self.K_bar = K_bar
        # Technology: Y = A * K^alpha * L^(1-alpha)
        # Note: alpha here is Capital share (standard notation), usually labor is 1-alpha.
        # Let's clarify: alpha_X is Capital share in X.
        self.alpha_X = alpha_X
        self.alpha_Y = alpha_Y
        self.A_X = A_X
        self.A_Y = A_Y
        # Preferences: U = X^beta * Y^(1-beta)
        self.beta_c = beta_c

    def unit_cost(self, W, R, sector):
        """Calculates unit cost using the dual cost function for Cobb-Douglas."""
        if sector == 'X':
            alpha, A = self.alpha_X, self.A_X
        else:
            alpha, A = self.alpha_Y, self.A_Y

        # Cost function c(w,r) = (1/A) * (r/alpha)^alpha * (w/(1-alpha))^(1-alpha)
        cost = (1/A) * ((R/alpha)**alpha) * ((W/(1-alpha))**(1-alpha))
        return cost

    def factor_demands_per_unit(self, W, R, sector):
        """Shephard's Lemma: dC/dw = L/Y, dC/dr = K/Y"""
        # For CD: w*L = (1-alpha)*C*Y => L/Y = (1-alpha)*C/w
        unit_c = self.unit_cost(W, R, sector)
        if sector == 'X':
            alpha = self.alpha_X
        else:
            alpha = self.alpha_Y

        l_unit = (1 - alpha) * unit_c / W
        k_unit = alpha * unit_c / R
        return l_unit, k_unit

    def excess_demand_system(self, vars):
        """
        System of equations to solve for equilibrium prices.
        Unknowns: vars = [P_X, P_Y, R] (Numeraire W=1)
        Equations: 
        1. P_X = UnitCost_X
        2. P_Y = UnitCost_Y
        3. Labor Market Clearing (or Capital)
        """
        P_X, P_Y, R = vars
        W = 1.0 # Numeraire

        # 1. Zero Profit Conditions
        cost_X = self.unit_cost(W, R, 'X')
        cost_Y = self.unit_cost(W, R, 'Y')
        eq1 = P_X - cost_X
        eq2 = P_Y - cost_Y

        # 2. Demand Side
        Income = W * self.L_bar + R * self.K_bar
        D_X = self.beta_c * Income / P_X
        D_Y = (1 - self.beta_c) * Income / P_Y

        # 3. Factor Demand
        lx, kx = self.factor_demands_per_unit(W, R, 'X')
        ly, ky = self.factor_demands_per_unit(W, R, 'Y')

        # Total Factor Demand
        L_demand = lx * D_X + ly * D_Y
        K_demand = kx * D_X + ky * D_Y

        # 4. Market Clearing Errors
        eq3 = self.L_bar - L_demand
        # eq4 = self.K_bar - K_demand # Redundant by Walras Law

        return [eq1, eq2, eq3]

    def solve_autarky(self):
        """Finds the autarky equilibrium."""
        # Initial guess: Prices approx 1, R approx 1
        guess = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
        sol = root(self.excess_demand_system, guess, method='lm')

        if not sol.success:
            print("Warning: Solver failed to converge.")

        self.P_X, self.P_Y, self.R = sol.x
        self.W = 1.0
        return pd.Series({'P_X': self.P_X, 'P_Y': self.P_Y, 'W': self.W, 'R': self.R})

    def get_excess_demand_goods(self, P_X, P_Y):
        """
        Calculates excess demand for goods X and Y given WORLD prices.
        To do this, we must solve for internal factor prices W, R consistent with these goods prices.
        """
        # Given P_X, P_Y, we need to find W, R that satisfy zero profit.
        # P_X = c_X(W, R)
        # P_Y = c_Y(W, R)
        # This is a 2x2 system for factor prices. (Stolper-Samuelson mapping)

        def factor_price_sys(vars):
            W, R = vars
            return [self.unit_cost(W, R, 'X') - P_X,
                    self.unit_cost(W, R, 'Y') - P_Y]

        try:
            f_sol = root(factor_price_sys, [1.0, 1.0], method='lm')
            if not f_sol.success:
                warnings.warn(f"Factor price solver did not converge for P_X={P_X:.4f}, P_Y={P_Y:.4f}")
            W, R = f_sol.x
        except (ValueError, RuntimeError) as e:
            warnings.warn(f"Factor price solver failed for P_X={P_X:.4f}, P_Y={P_Y:.4f}: {e}")
            return np.array([np.nan, np.nan])

        # Now calculate supply and demand at these factor prices
        # Supply is determined by factor endowments and optimal K/L ratios.
        lx, kx = self.factor_demands_per_unit(W, R, 'X')
        ly, ky = self.factor_demands_per_unit(W, R, 'Y')

        # Solve linear system for outputs Y_X, Y_Y:
        # lx * Y_X + ly * Y_Y = L_bar
        # kx * Y_X + ky * Y_Y = K_bar
        A_mat = np.array([[lx, ly], [kx, ky]])
        b_vec = np.array([self.L_bar, self.K_bar])
        try:
            outputs = np.linalg.solve(A_mat, b_vec)
            Y_X, Y_Y = outputs
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
             # Outside Cone of Diversification (specialization)
             warnings.warn(f"Singular factor demand matrix at P_X={P_X:.4f}, P_Y={P_Y:.4f}. Outside cone of diversification.")
             return np.array([0.0, 0.0])

        # Demand
        Income = W * self.L_bar + R * self.K_bar
        D_X = self.beta_c * Income / P_X
        D_Y = (1 - self.beta_c) * Income / P_Y

        return np.array([D_X - Y_X, D_Y - Y_Y])

# --- Instantiate and Solve for Autarky ---
usa = CGEModel(L_bar=100, K_bar=100, alpha_X=0.3, alpha_Y=0.7) # Balanced
res = usa.solve_autarky()

print("> **Note:** Autarky Equilibrium Found:")
display(res.to_frame('Value'))

    3. The Heckscher-Ohlin Model of Trade¶

    We now introduce a second country. - Home: Labor Abundant (\(L=100, K=50\)). - Foreign: Capital Abundant (\(L=50, K=100\)). - Technology: Identical in both countries.

    Heckscher-Ohlin Theorem: A country will export the good that uses its abundant factor intensively. - Sector X is Labor Intensive (\(\alpha_X=0.3\) is capital share, so labor share is 0.7). - Sector Y is Capital Intensive (\(\alpha_Y=0.7\) is capital share).

    Prediction: Home (Labor abundant) should export X (Labor intensive).

    class HeckscherOhlinSystem:
    def __init__(self, home_model, foreign_model):
        self.home = home_model
        self.foreign = foreign_model

    def world_excess_demand(self, prices):
        # We solve for relative price P = P_X / P_Y
        # Let P_Y = 1 (Numeraire for world trade)
        P_X = prices[0]
        P_Y = 1.0

        ed_home = self.home.get_excess_demand_goods(P_X, P_Y)
        ed_fgn = self.foreign.get_excess_demand_goods(P_X, P_Y)

        # Return global excess demand for X (Walras Law handles Y)
        return ed_home[0] + ed_fgn[0]

# Setup Countries
home = CGEModel(L_bar=100, K_bar=50, alpha_X=0.3, alpha_Y=0.7)  # Labor Rich
foreign = CGEModel(L_bar=50, K_bar=100, alpha_X=0.3, alpha_Y=0.7) # Capital Rich

# 1. Check Autarky Prices
p_home = home.solve_autarky()
p_fgn = foreign.solve_autarky()
rel_p_home = p_home['P_X'] / p_home['P_Y']
rel_p_fgn = p_fgn['P_X'] / p_fgn['P_Y']

print(f"> **Note:** Autarky Relative Price (Px/Py): Home={rel_p_home:.3f}, Foreign={rel_p_fgn:.3f}")
print(f"> **Observation:** Good X is cheaper in Home ({rel_p_home:.3f} < {rel_p_fgn:.3f}), creating an incentive to export it.")

# 2. Solve for Free Trade Equilibrium
ho_sys = HeckscherOhlinSystem(home, foreign)

# Root finding for World Price P_X (fixing P_Y=1)
sol_trade = root(ho_sys.world_excess_demand, [1.0], method='lm')
world_Px = sol_trade.x[0]

print(f"\n> **Note:** World Equilibrium Price Px (Py=1): {world_Px:.3f}")

# 3. Analyze Trade Flows
ed_home_trade = home.get_excess_demand_goods(world_Px, 1.0)
ed_fgn_trade = foreign.get_excess_demand_goods(world_Px, 1.0)

print(f"> **Trade Flow:** Home Exports X: {-ed_home_trade[0]:.3f} units (Negative Excess Demand = Supply)")
print(f"> **Trade Flow:** Foreign Imports X: {ed_fgn_trade[0]:.3f} units")

    Key Equations¶

    These relations are collected from the derivations above as a review map. Their assumptions and derivations remain part of the result; this box is not a substitute for them.

    1. Core relation

    \[MRS_A \;=\; \frac{\partial u_A/\partial x}{\partial u_A/\partial y} \;=\; \frac{\partial u_B/\partial x}{\partial u_B/\partial y} \;=\; MRS_B.\]

    2. Core relation

    \[MRS = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{y}{x}.\]

    3. Core relation

    \[k_A \frac{y_A}{x_A} = k_B \frac{\bar{y} - y_A}{\bar{x} - x_A} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; k_A\, y_A (\bar{x} - x_A) = k_B\, (\bar{y} - y_A)\, x_A.\]

    4. Core relation

    \[y_A(x_A) \;=\; \frac{k_B\, \bar{y}\, x_A}{k_A(\bar{x} - x_A) + k_B\, x_A}.\]

    Summary¶

    In this notebook, we moved from the abstract theory of the "invisible hand" to a concrete, computational implementation.

    1. CGE Modeling: We built a solver that finds the unique price vector clearing all markets simultaneously. This is the core algorithm used in policy institutions worldwide.
    2. Heckscher-Ohlin: We verified the classic trade theorem. The labor-abundant country had a lower autarky price for the labor-intensive good and became an exporter of that good under free trade.
    3. Gains from Trade: Trade equalized goods prices. While not explicitly shown here, this convergence typically benefits aggregate welfare, though it creates winners and losers (Stolper-Samuelson theorem) within each country.

    This framework can be easily extended to \(N\) sectors and households, forming the basis of modern trade policy analysis.
