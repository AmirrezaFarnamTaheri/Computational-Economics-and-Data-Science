    The Lens: Universal Function Approximation¶

    What problem are we solving? Many economic relationships are highly non-linear and interactive. Traditional models struggle with image data, text, and high-dimensional feature spaces. Neural networks are universal function approximators: with enough neurons, they can learn any continuous mapping from inputs to outputs.

    Why this method? Deep learning has revolutionized prediction in domains from computer vision to NLP. For economists, it offers powerful tools for nowcasting, satellite image analysis, and learning complex policy functions in structural models.

    Economic question. In 06 Deep Learning Foundations, what must remain economically invariant when the computational representation changes? For economists, predictive performance is useful but not sufficient. The model must be evaluated against the decision or forecasting problem, the information set available at prediction time, and the cost of distribution shift or leakage. Ask what inductive bias the method introduces, how tuning choices are validated out of sample, and which errors matter economically. When the goal is causal or structural, prediction should be treated as a nuisance component rather than evidence of identification by itself.

    Learning Objectives¶

    • Build feedforward neural networks from scratch and with PyTorch/TensorFlow.
    • Explain backpropagation, gradient descent, and the role of activation functions.
    • Tune architectures (depth, width, dropout, batch normalization) to avoid overfitting.
    • Train models on economic datasets and interpret learned representations.

    Introduction¶

    Deep Learning refers to a class of machine learning models based on artificial neural networks with many layers (hence "deep"). For economists, it is useful to think of deep learning not as a mysterious black box, but as a powerful new toolkit for highly flexible, non-parametric function approximation. These models have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a vast range of tasks, particularly those involving high-dimensional or unstructured data (like images, text, and geospatial data) where traditional econometric methods may struggle due to restrictive parametric assumptions.

    This notebook provides a rigorous, PhD-level introduction to the core concepts of deep learning, focusing on the theoretical underpinnings and the practical considerations for economic applications. We will dissect the optimization algorithms that make training possible, explore the key architectural families (MLPs, CNNs, RNNs), and delve into the frontier of integrating deep learning with causal inference and model interpretability.

    Theoretical Foundations¶

    1.1 The Universal Approximation Theorem¶

    The Lens promised that neural networks are "universal function approximators." That is not marketing — it is a theorem, and it is worth stating precisely, because both what it does and what it does not say shape how we use these models in practice.

    Setup. Consider the simplest possible network: a single hidden layer of \(N\) neurons, each applying an activation function \(\varphi\) to an affine function of the input, with outputs combined linearly. Such a network computes

    \[ G(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \, \varphi\!\left( w_i^\top x + b_i \right), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^n, \]

    where \(w_i \in \mathbb{R}^n\) are the input weights, \(b_i \in \mathbb{R}\) the biases, and \(\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}\) the output weights. Call \(\varphi\) sigmoidal if \(\varphi(t) \to 1\) as \(t \to +\infty\) and \(\varphi(t) \to 0\) as \(t \to -\infty\) (the logistic function \(1/(1+e^{-t})\) and \(\tanh\), rescaled, both qualify).

    Theorem (Universal Approximation; Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991). Let \(\varphi\) be any continuous sigmoidal function and let \(I_n = [0,1]^n\) be the unit hypercube. Then finite sums of the form \(G(x)\) above are dense in \(C(I_n)\), the space of continuous functions on \(I_n\), under the sup norm. That is: for any continuous \(f : I_n \to \mathbb{R}\) and any \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exist \(N\), and parameters \(\{\alpha_i, w_i, b_i\}_{i=1}^N\), such that $$ \sup_{x \in I_n} \big| G(x) - f(x) \big| \;<\; \varepsilon. $$

    Hornik (1991) strengthened this considerably: the result holds for any continuous, bounded, non-constant activation — the magic is not in the sigmoid's shape, it is in the layered architecture combined with any nonlinearity at all. (For the ReLU \(\varphi(t) = \max(0,t)\), which is unbounded, an analogous theorem holds by a separate argument.)

    Proof sketch — why it is true, in one dimension. The mechanism is easiest to see for \(n = 1\), and the general case is the same idea run in higher dimensions.

    Step 1: a single neuron approximates a step. Take the logistic \(\varphi\) and consider \(\varphi(w(x - c))\) for large \(w\). As \(w \to \infty\), this converges pointwise to the Heaviside step \(\mathbb{1}\{x > c\}\): the sigmoid's transition region has width \(O(1/w)\), so cranking \(w\) up makes the "S" arbitrarily steep. One neuron \(\approx\) one step function at location \(c\).

    Step 2: two neurons approximate a bump. Subtracting two steps at nearby locations \(c\) and \(c + h\) gives an indicator of an interval:

    \[ \varphi\big(w(x - c)\big) - \varphi\big(w(x - c - h)\big) \;\xrightarrow[w \to \infty]{}\; \mathbb{1}\{c < x \le c+h\}. \]

    So a pair of hidden neurons with output weights \(+1\) and \(-1\) builds a rectangular bump of width \(h\) anywhere we like.

    Step 3: sum bumps into a staircase. Partition \([0,1]\) into \(M\) intervals of width \(h = 1/M\). For each interval, add a bump scaled by \(f\) evaluated at that interval's left endpoint. The resulting network output is a step-function approximation of \(f\):

    \[ G(x) \;\approx\; \sum_{j=1}^{M} f(c_j)\, \mathbb{1}\{c_j < x \le c_j + h\}. \]

    Step 4: invoke uniform continuity. \(f\) is continuous on the compact set \([0,1]\), hence uniformly continuous: for any \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) with \(|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(|x - y| < \delta\). Choose \(M\) large enough that \(h = 1/M < \delta\). Then on each interval, the staircase's constant value \(f(c_j)\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(f(x)\) for every \(x\) in that interval — so the staircase is uniformly within \(\varepsilon\) of \(f\). Choosing \(w\) large enough makes the sigmoid-based bumps approximate the exact indicators to within any further tolerance. Combining the two error sources gives \(\sup_x |G(x) - f(x)| < 2\varepsilon\), and since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, the claim follows. \(\blacksquare\) (This construction needs \(2M\) neurons; Cybenko's actual proof is non-constructive and far slicker — it argues by contradiction using the Hahn–Banach theorem and the Riesz representation theorem to show that if the closure of these sums were a proper subspace of \(C(I_n)\), there would exist a nonzero signed measure annihilating every sigmoid, which is impossible.)

    What the theorem does NOT say — three critical caveats. This is where practitioners go wrong, so read carefully:

    The theorem says The theorem does not say
    A network of some width \(N\) exists that approximates \(f\) How large \(N\) must be. It can be exponential in the input dimension \(n\) — the curse of dimensionality is not repealed
    Such parameters \(\{\alpha_i, w_i, b_i\}\) exist That gradient descent will find them. Existence is not learnability; the loss surface is non-convex
    \(f\) can be approximated on a compact set Anything about extrapolation outside the training domain, where networks are notoriously unreliable

    There is also a fourth, quieter caveat: the theorem is about approximating a known function \(f\), whereas in practice we only see noisy samples of it. Approximation power that is "too" universal is exactly what enables overfitting — a sufficiently wide network can interpolate the noise as happily as the signal, which is why the regularization techniques later in this notebook (dropout, weight decay, early stopping) are not optional extras but load-bearing.

    Why depth, then? If one hidden layer already suffices in principle, why is the field called deep learning? Because the theorem is about existence, not efficiency. For many function classes, a deep network represents \(f\) with exponentially fewer neurons than any shallow network achieving the same accuracy (Telgarsky 2016; Eldan & Shamir 2016 give explicit separations). Depth buys parameter efficiency and a hierarchy of reusable intermediate features — the practical reason architectures got deeper rather than merely wider.

    The economist's reading. The UAT is the formal license for treating a neural network as a non-parametric estimator: we are not assuming a functional form, we are letting the data choose one from a class rich enough to contain (arbitrarily good approximations of) anything continuous. That is the same intellectual move as kernel regression or series estimators in econometrics, and it comes with the same trade-off — flexibility purchased at the cost of interpretability, extrapolation safety, and a much greater burden on regularization and validation to avoid fitting noise.

    1.2 Backpropagation: The Engine of Learning¶

    Intellectual Provenance: While the chain rule is centuries old, its efficient application to neural networks, known as backpropagation, has a rich history. The core ideas were developed independently by several researchers in the 1960s and 70s. However, it was the 1986 paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors," that popularized the method and demonstrated its power for training deep networks, cementing it as the foundational algorithm for the field.

    Backpropagation is simply a computationally efficient algorithm for applying the chain rule of calculus to compute the gradients of the loss function with respect to every weight in the network. These gradients are then used by an optimization algorithm (like SGD or Adam) to update the weights.

    # --- Backpropagation from Scratch (Numpy) ---

# Simple Neural Net: Input (2) -> Hidden (3) -> Output (1)
# Activation: Sigmoid

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

def sigmoid_derivative(x):
    return x * (1 - x)

# Initialize Weights
input_size = 2
hidden_size = 3
output_size = 1

W1 = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (input_size, hidden_size))
b1 = np.zeros((1, hidden_size))
W2 = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (hidden_size, output_size))
b2 = np.zeros((1, output_size))

# XOR Problem
X = np.array([[0,0], [0,1], [1,0], [1,1]])
y = np.array([[0], [1], [1], [0]])

lr = 0.1
epochs = 5000

loss_history = []

print("Training Neural Network on XOR...")
for epoch in range(epochs):
    # Forward Pass
    layer1_input = np.dot(X, W1) + b1
    layer1_output = sigmoid(layer1_input)

    layer2_input = np.dot(layer1_output, W2) + b2
    output = sigmoid(layer2_input)

    # Loss (MSE)
    loss = np.mean(0.5 * (y - output)**2)
    loss_history.append(loss)

    # Backward Pass (Chain Rule)
    # dLoss/dOutput = -(y - output)
    error = y - output
    d_output = error * sigmoid_derivative(output)

    error_layer1 = d_output.dot(W2.T)
    d_layer1 = error_layer1 * sigmoid_derivative(layer1_output)

    # Update Weights
    W2 += layer1_output.T.dot(d_output) * lr
    b2 += np.sum(d_output, axis=0, keepdims=True) * lr
    W1 += X.T.dot(d_layer1) * lr
    b1 += np.sum(d_layer1, axis=0, keepdims=True) * lr

print(f"Final Loss: {loss:.6f}")
print("Predictions vs Truth:")
print(np.hstack([output, y]))

plt.plot(loss_history)
plt.xlabel("Epochs")
plt.ylabel("MSE Loss")
plt.title("Training Curve: Solving XOR with Backprop")
plt.show()

    Predicting House Prices with a Regularized MLP¶

    if TENSORFLOW_AVAILABLE:
    # 1. Load and Preprocess Data
    # California Housing is a standard regression benchmark (replaces deprecated Boston Housing).
    # Features are scaled to zero mean and unit variance for numerical stability.
    from sklearn.datasets import fetch_california_housing
    housing = fetch_california_housing()
    x_train, x_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
        housing.data, housing.target, test_size=0.2, random_state=42
    )
    feature_names = housing.feature_names
    scaler = StandardScaler()
    x_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(x_train)
    x_test_scaled = scaler.transform(x_test)

    # 2. Define the Model Architecture
    # We use a standard MLP with two hidden layers of 64 neurons each.
    # - 'relu' activation is chosen for its efficiency and ability to mitigate vanishing gradients.
    # - Dropout layers are added as a regularization technique. By randomly setting 20% of
    #   neuron outputs to zero during training, we prevent the model from becoming too
    #   reliant on any single neuron, which helps prevent overfitting.
    # - The final layer has a single neuron with no activation (linear) for the regression output.
    model = keras.Sequential([
        keras.Input(shape=(x_train.shape[1],)),
        layers.Dense(64, activation='relu'),
        layers.Dropout(0.2),
        layers.Dense(64, activation='relu'),
        layers.Dropout(0.2),
        layers.Dense(1)
    ])

    # 3. Compile and Train
    # - 'adam' optimizer is a robust choice that adapts the learning rate for each parameter.
    # - 'mean_squared_error' is the standard loss function for regression problems.
    model.compile(optimizer='adam', loss='mean_squared_error')
    # We train for 150 epochs, using 20% of the training data for validation to monitor performance.
    print("Training MLP on California Housing Data...")
    model.fit(x_train_scaled, y_train, epochs=150, validation_split=0.2, verbose=0)

    # 4. Evaluate Performance
    loss = model.evaluate(x_test_scaled, y_test, verbose=0)
    print(f"\n--- Model Performance on Test Set ---")
    print(f"Mean Squared Error: {loss:.2f}")

    Common Pitfalls in This Lecture

    • Raw inputs into a network. Feeding unscaled features forces one learning rate to serve features measured in dollars and features measured in fractions — slow, divergent optimization. Standardize inputs (fit on train only) as a reflex.
    • Training loss as evidence. Training loss falling says nothing about generalization; small-sample econometric applications overfit almost immediately. Track a validation curve, regularize (weight decay, dropout, early stopping) and report validation metrics.
