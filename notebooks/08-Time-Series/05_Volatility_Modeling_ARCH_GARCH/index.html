    Historical Context — Engle 1982, Bollerslev 1986. Rob Engle's ARCH model (Econometrica 1982) captured volatility clustering in UK inflation after noticing big shocks cluster in returns; Tim Bollerslev's GARCH (1986) generalized it into finance's risk-management standard — Value-at-Risk desks run on their insight. Shared 2003 Nobel with Granger.

    The Lens: The Pulse of Fear¶

    In classical economics, risk is often treated as a constant parameter. In financial markets, risk breathes. It spikes during crises, sleeps during booms, and clusters in time. If the market crashes today, it is highly likely to be volatile tomorrow. This phenomenon is called Volatility Clustering.

    Standard regression models (like OLS) fail here because they assume constant variance (homoskedasticity). To price options, manage risk, or forecast value-at-risk (VaR), we need models that treat volatility as a dynamic process.

    This notebook introduces the Nobel Prize-winning ARCH (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) and GARCH models.

    Economic question. In 05 Volatility Modeling ARCH GARCH, what must remain economically invariant when the computational representation changes? Time-series methods exploit dependence across dates, so chronology is part of the data-generating process rather than a nuisance index. The central question is what can be learned from persistence, innovations, and cross-variable dynamics without leaking future information into the past. Model adequacy must therefore be judged through residual diagnostics, stability, and genuinely out-of-sample forecasting or structural restrictions.

    Learning Objectives¶

    • Estimate GARCH(1,1) models and interpret the persistence parameters.

    • Apply conditional volatility forecasts to risk management (Value-at-Risk).

    1. The Stylized Facts of Financial Returns¶

    Figure: Simulated GARCH(1,1) returns and conditional volatility..

    Financial time series exhibit three key properties that break standard Gaussian assumptions: 1. Absence of Autocorrelation: Returns are hard to predict (\(E[r_{t+1}|r_t] \approx 0\)). 2. Volatility Clustering: Squared returns are highly autocorrelated. Large moves follow large moves. 3. Fat Tails (Leptokurtosis): Extreme events (6-sigma moves) happen far more often than a Normal distribution allows.

    # Load Local Data
try:
    sp500 = pd.read_csv('../data/sp500.csv', index_col='Date', parse_dates=True)
    # Check if 'Close' or 'Adj Close' exists
    if 'Adj Close' in sp500.columns:
        prices = sp500['Adj Close']
    else:
        prices = sp500['Close']

    # Calculate Returns (Scale by 100 for numerical stability in optimizer)
    returns = 100 * prices.pct_change().dropna()
    print("> **Note:** Loaded S&P 500 data from local storage.")

except FileNotFoundError:
    print("Error: '../data/sp500.csv' not found. Generating synthetic GARCH process.")
    n = 2000
    w = 0.05
    alpha = 0.1
    beta = 0.85
    y = np.zeros(n)
    sigma2 = np.zeros(n)
    sigma2[0] = w / (1 - alpha - beta)
    for t in range(1, n):
        sigma2[t] = w + alpha * y[t-1]**2 + beta * sigma2[t-1]
        y[t] = np.sqrt(sigma2[t]) * rng.normal()

    returns = pd.Series(y, index=pd.date_range('2015-01-01', periods=n, freq='D'))
    returns.name = 'Synthetic Returns'

# Plot Returns
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))
returns.plot(alpha=0.6, linewidth=0.8)
plt.title('Daily Returns: Volatility Clustering in Action')
plt.ylabel('Percentage Return')
plt.show()

    1.1 Visualizing Fat Tails¶

    We compare the empirical distribution of returns against a Normal distribution with the same mean and variance. The "peaked" center and "heavy" tails are classic signs of non-normality.

    fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))

# Histogram
mu, std = stats.norm.fit(returns)
ax1.hist(returns, bins=80, density=True, alpha=0.6, color='steelblue', label='Actual Returns')
xmin, xmax = ax1.get_xlim()
x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, 100)
p = stats.norm.pdf(x, mu, std)
ax1.plot(x, p, 'k', linewidth=2, label='Normal Dist')
ax1.set_title('Distribution of Returns vs. Normal')
ax1.legend()

# Q-Q Plot
stats.probplot(returns, dist="norm", plot=ax2)
ax2.set_title('Q-Q Plot (Normal Probability Plot)')
plt.show()

    2. The GARCH(1,1) Model¶

    The standard GARCH(1,1) specification is:

    1. Mean Equation: \(r_t = \mu + \epsilon_t\)
    2. Variance Equation: \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2\)

    Where: - \(\alpha\) captures the reaction to new shocks (ARCH term). - \(\beta\) captures the persistence of old volatility (GARCH term). - With unit-variance innovations, \(\omega>0\), \(\alpha,\beta\geq0\), and \(\alpha+\beta<1\) give finite unconditional variance \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\). Strict stationarity has a different log-moment condition.

    # Fit GARCH(1,1)
model = arch_model(returns, mean='Constant', vol='GARCH', p=1, q=1)
res = model.fit(disp='off')

print(res.summary())

# Extract Conditional Volatility
cond_vol = res.conditional_volatility

# Plot
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))
plt.plot(cond_vol, color='red', linewidth=1)
plt.title('Estimated Daily Conditional Volatility')
plt.ylabel('Daily Volatility (%)')
plt.show()

    3. Asymmetry: The Leverage Effect¶

    Markets often crash faster than they recover. Bad news increases volatility more than good news. The standard GARCH model is symmetric (it squares the residuals). The GJR-GARCH model adds an interaction term to capture this:

    \[ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \gamma I_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 \]

    where \(I_{t-1} = 1\) if \(\epsilon_{t-1} < 0\). A positive \(\gamma\) implies the leverage effect.

    # Fit GJR-GARCH(1,1)
# o=1 adds the asymmetry term
model_gjr = arch_model(returns, mean='Constant', vol='GARCH', p=1, o=1, q=1)
res_gjr = model_gjr.fit(disp='off')

print("> **Note:** GJR-GARCH Parameter Estimates:")
print(res_gjr.params)

gamma_val = res_gjr.params.get('gamma[1]', 0)
if gamma_val > 0:
    print(f"\n> **Observation:** Gamma is positive ({gamma_val:.4f}). The fitted model assigns a larger response to negative shocks; inspect its confidence interval before claiming statistical evidence.")
else:
    print("\n> **Observation:** The fitted asymmetry coefficient is nonpositive; its sign alone is not a significance test.")

    Common Pitfalls in This Lecture

    • GARCH without a mean model. Fitting GARCH to raw returns leaves predictable mean dynamics in \(u_t\), contaminating the variance equation. Specify and estimate the mean equation (often just a constant) jointly, then check standardized residuals for remaining structure.
    • Explosive variance constraints. Without \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta < 1\), fitted models forecast exploding variance (IGARCH at the boundary). Impose the constraints and inspect persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) before projecting volatility paths.

    4. Summary¶

    1. Volatility is Predictable: Even if returns are random, risk is not. It clusters.
    2. GARCH: This model captures the memory of risk. High \(\beta\) values (typically > 0.9) indicate that market fear dissipates slowly.
    3. Risk Management: By forecasting \(\sigma_{t+1}\), we can construct better Value-at-Risk (VaR) models than by simply assuming a constant historical standard deviation.
