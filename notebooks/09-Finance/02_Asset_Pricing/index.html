    1. The Unifying Theory: The Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF)¶

    Asset pricing theory answers one fundamental question: what is the fair price of a future, uncertain cash flow? The answer is always the same: the price is the expected discounted value of the payoff.

    The fundamental pricing equation is: $$ p_t = E_t[m_{t+1} x_{t+1}] $$

    where: - \(p_t\) is the asset price today. - \(x_{t+1}\) is the future random payoff (price + dividend). - \(m_{t+1}\) is the Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF).

    The SDF is the key. It is a random variable that tells us how much we value a dollar in each possible future state. It is high in "bad states" (when we are poor/hungry) and low in "good states" (when we are rich). This means assets that pay off in bad times (insurance) are valuable (high price, low return), while assets that pay off in good times (tech stocks) are less valuable (low price, high return).

    The Lens: The Price of Risk¶

    What economic problem are we solving? Why does an average stock return 8% a year while a Treasury bill returns 2%? Why do value stocks outperform growth stocks? Is it free money, or compensation for risk? To answer this, we need a theory of valuation. We need to know what makes an asset "risky" in the eyes of a marginal investor.

    Why do we need this method? Asset pricing is a jungle of acronyms: CAPM, APT, CCAPM, ICAPM. But beneath this complexity lies a single, beautiful unifying idea: the Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF). The price of any asset is the expected value of its payoff discounted by the SDF (\(P = E[MX]\)). This notebook strips away the jargon to build this unifying framework from first principles, showing how every major asset pricing model is just a different assumption about \(M\).

    Economic question. In 02 Asset Pricing, what must remain economically invariant when the computational representation changes? Financial models connect prices to risk, timing, and no-arbitrage restrictions. The economic question is which states of the world make a payoff valuable, how risk is transferred or hedged, and how sensitive the valuation is to assumptions about dynamics and market completeness. A numerical price is credible only when it respects basic bounds, limiting cases, and an independent replication or hedging argument.

    2. Model 1: The Consumption-Based CAPM (CCAPM)¶

    If we assume power utility, \(u(C) = \frac{C^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), then \(m_{t+1} = \beta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma}\). This gives the CCAPM: $$ E_t[R_i] - R_f \approx \gamma \cdot \text{Cov}(R_i, \Delta c_{t+1}) $$ Risk is defined as covariance with consumption growth. This model is theoretically elegant but fails empirically. Consumption is too smooth to explain the volatility of stock returns, leading to the Equity Premium Puzzle.

    Dimension notes: payoffs, prices and the SDF \(m_{t+1}\) are scalars; consumption growth \(C_{t+1}/C_t\) scalar; the CCAPM pricing relation binds two scalar moments through covariance with \(\gamma > 0\) risk aversion.

    The Equity Premium Puzzle and the Hansen-Jagannathan Bound¶

    Hansen and Jagannathan (1991) showed that for any valid SDF, its volatility must satisfy a lower bound determined by the Sharpe Ratio of the market: $$ \frac{\sigma(m)}{E[m]} \ge \frac{|E[R_m] - R_f|}{\sigma(R_m)} $$

    We can visualize this bound. The CCAPM SDF (based on smooth consumption) lies far below the bound unless risk aversion \(\gamma\) is implausibly high (\(>50\)).

    ### Visualizing the Hansen-Jagannathan Bound
def plot_hj_bound():
    # Market statistics (annualized)
    market_return = 0.08
    risk_free_rate = 0.02
    market_std = 0.16
    sharpe_ratio = (market_return - risk_free_rate) / market_std

    # Consumption growth statistics
    cons_growth_std = 0.02

    # Plot HJ Bound
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 6))
    mean_m = np.linspace(0.95, 1.05, 100)
    hj_bound = sharpe_ratio * mean_m
    ax.plot(mean_m, hj_bound, 'r-', lw=2, label='Hansen-Jagannathan Bound')
    ax.fill_between(mean_m, hj_bound, 3, color='red', alpha=0.1, label='Admissible Region for SDF')

    # Plot CCAPM SDF for different gamma
    gammas = [1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 50]
    for gamma in gammas:
        # Approximate E[m] and std(m) from CCAPM formula
        mean_m_ccapm = 1 / (1 + risk_free_rate)
        std_m_ccapm = gamma * cons_growth_std * mean_m_ccapm
        ax.scatter(mean_m_ccapm, std_m_ccapm, s=80, zorder=5)
        ax.annotate(f'γ={gamma}', (mean_m_ccapm, std_m_ccapm), xytext=(5,5), textcoords='offset points')

    ax.set_xlabel('Mean of SDF, E[m]')
    ax.set_ylabel('Std. Dev. of SDF, σ(m)')
    ax.set_title('The Equity Premium Puzzle: CCAPM vs. HJ Bound')
    ax.legend()
    ax.set_xlim(0.95, 1.02)
    ax.set_ylim(0, 0.8)
    plt.show() # Render plot

plot_hj_bound()

    3. Model 2: The Capital Asset Pricing Model¶

    Figure: The two-pass Fama-MacBeth estimation procedure.. (CAPM)

    Since consumption data is flawed, finance turned to Factor Models. The CAPM assumes the SDF is linear in the market return: \(m_{t+1} = a - b R_{m, t+1}\). This implies: $$ E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_i (E[R_m] - R_f) $$ where \(\beta_i\) measures sensitivity to the market. The CAPM predicts that market beta is the only risk that matters.

    Dimension notes: market return \(R_{m,t+1}\) scalar; SDF affine in it makes \(\beta_i\) a scalar regression slope; the CAPM restriction is that intercepts vanish asset-by-asset.

    4. Model 3: The Fama-French Five-Factor Model¶

    Empirically, the CAPM fails. Small stocks and value stocks earn higher returns than their betas predict. Fama and French (1993, 2015) augmented the CAPM with size (SMB), value (HML), profitability (RMW), and investment (CMA) factors: $$ R_i - R_f = \alpha_i + \beta_{mkt}(R_m-R_f) + \beta_{smb}SMB + \beta_{hml}HML + \beta_{rmw}RMW + \beta_{cma}CMA + \epsilon_i $$

    5. Testing Asset Pricing Models: Fama-MacBeth Regressions¶

    To test if these factors are actually "priced" (i.e., carry a risk premium), we use the Fama-MacBeth (1973) two-step procedure: 1. Time-Series Regression: Run time-series regressions for each asset \(i\) to estimate its betas (\(\beta_{i,k}\)) on the factors. 2. Cross-Sectional Regression: For each time period \(t\), run a cross-sectional regression of asset returns \(R_{i,t}\) on the estimated betas. The slope coefficients \(\lambda_{k,t}\) are the risk premia. 3. T-Test: Test if the time-series average of \(\lambda_{k,t}\) is significantly different from zero.

    Time-Series Regressions (First Pass)¶

    The first stage of Fama–MacBeth runs a separate time-series regression of each test asset's excess return on the factors: \(R_{it}^e = \alpha_i + \beta_i' f_t + \varepsilon_{it}\). This estimates each asset's factor loadings (the betas) and its unexplained return (the pricing error \(\alpha_i\)). Only the loadings carry into the second stage.

    6. Unified Case Study: Testing Factor Models¶

    We will download real data from Ken French's library and test the CAPM vs. the Fama-French 5-Factor model using 10 Industry Portfolios as our test assets.

    Data Collection¶

    The case study needs monthly excess returns for test portfolios and for each factor. Factor returns come from the published Fama–French data library and the risk-free rate from the same source; test assets are long-window portfolios whose returns are aligned to the factor frequency. Everything is merged on the date index before estimation begins.

    ### Data Collection
DATA_LOADED_SUCCESS = False
if PD_READER_AVAILABLE:
    print("> **Note:** Attempting to download Fama-French data...")
    try:
        # Download FF5 Factors
        ff_factors_raw = web.DataReader('F-F_Research_Data_5_Factors_2x3', 'famafrench', start='1963-07-01')
        ff_factors = ff_factors_raw[0] / 100 # Monthly data
        ff_factors.index = ff_factors.index.to_timestamp()

        # Download 10 Industry Portfolios
        ind_port_raw = web.DataReader('10_Industry_Portfolios', 'famafrench', start='1963-07-01')
        industry_portfolios = ind_port_raw[0] / 100
        industry_portfolios.index = industry_portfolios.index.to_timestamp()

        print("> **Note:** Data downloaded successfully.")
        DATA_LOADED_SUCCESS = True
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"> **Note:** Download failed ({e}). Case study skipped.")

if DATA_LOADED_SUCCESS:
    ff_factors.rename(columns={'Mkt-RF': 'MKT'}, inplace=True)
    # Calculate excess returns
    excess_returns = industry_portfolios.subtract(ff_factors['RF'], axis=0)
    display(ff_factors.head())

    ### Time-Series Regressions (First Pass)
def get_factor_betas(excess_returns, factors):
    """Runs time-series regressions to get factor betas for each asset."""
    betas = {}
    X = sm.add_constant(factors)
    for portfolio in excess_returns.columns:
        y = excess_returns[portfolio]
        # Align data indices
        common_index = y.index.intersection(X.index)
        if len(common_index) < 10: continue
        model = sm.OLS(y.loc[common_index], X.loc[common_index]).fit()
        betas[portfolio] = model.params
    return pd.DataFrame(betas).T

if DATA_LOADED_SUCCESS:
    # CAPM Betas
    capm_betas = get_factor_betas(excess_returns, ff_factors[['MKT']])

    # Fama-French 5-Factor Betas
    ff5_factors = ff_factors[['MKT', 'SMB', 'HML', 'RMW', 'CMA']]
    ff5_betas = get_factor_betas(excess_returns, ff5_factors)

    print("> **Note:** Fama-French 5-Factor Betas (First Pass Results)")
    display(ff5_betas.head())

    ### Fama-MacBeth Regressions (Second Pass)
def run_fama_macbeth(excess_returns, betas, lags=6):
    """Performs Fama-MacBeth regressions with Newey-West correction."""
    # Drop alpha (const) from betas, we only want factor loadings
    X_betas = betas.drop(columns=['const'], errors='ignore')

    # Cross-sectional regressions for each time period
    lambdas = []
    for t in excess_returns.index:
        y_t = excess_returns.loc[t]
        # Regress returns on betas to get risk premia
        # Add constant to estimate zero-beta rate
        X_t = sm.add_constant(X_betas)

        # Align assets
        assets = y_t.index.intersection(X_t.index)
        if len(assets) > 2:
            model = sm.OLS(y_t[assets], X_t.loc[assets]).fit()
            lambdas.append(model.params)

    lambdas_df = pd.DataFrame(lambdas, index=excess_returns.index)

    # Time-series average and t-stats
    avg_lambdas = lambdas_df.mean()
    t_stats = {}
    p_values = {}

    for col in lambdas_df.columns:
        series = lambdas_df[col].dropna()
        if len(series) > 10:
            # Newey-West standard errors
            model = sm.OLS(series, sm.add_constant(np.ones(len(series)))).fit(cov_type='HAC', cov_kwds={'maxlags': lags})
            t_stats[col] = model.tvalues[0]
            p_values[col] = model.pvalues[0]

    results = pd.DataFrame({
        'Risk Premium': avg_lambdas,
        'T-Stat': pd.Series(t_stats),
        'P-Value': pd.Series(p_values)
    })
    return results

if DATA_LOADED_SUCCESS:
    ff5_results = run_fama_macbeth(excess_returns, ff5_betas)
    print("> **Note:** Fama-MacBeth Test Results for FF5 Model")
    display(ff5_results)

    Analysis and Interpretation¶

    The results above show the estimated risk premium (\(\lambda\)) for each factor. A statistically significant t-statistic (\(>1.96\)) indicates that the factor is "priced"—investors demand compensation for bearing that risk.

    • MKT: Usually priced, confirming the CAPM intuition.
    • HML (Value): Often priced, suggesting value stocks are indeed riskier.
    • SMB (Size): Evidence is often weaker for the size effect in modern samples.
    • const: Should be zero if the model holds perfectly (zero-beta rate equals risk-free rate).

    Key Equations¶

    These relations are collected from the derivations above as a review map. Their assumptions and derivations remain part of the result; this box is not a substitute for them.

    1. Core relation

    \[p_t = E_t[m_{t+1} x_{t+1}]\]

    2. Core relation

    \[E_t[R_i] - R_f \approx \gamma \cdot \text{Cov}(R_i, \Delta c_{t+1})\]

    3. Core relation

    \[\frac{\sigma(m)}{E[m]} \ge \frac{|E[R_m] - R_f|}{\sigma(R_m)}\]

    4. Core relation

    \[E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_i (E[R_m] - R_f)\]

    Dimension notes: \(p_t\), payoff \(x_{t+1}\), SDF \(m_{t+1}\) all scalar; excess-return relations hold per asset \(i\); factor exposures are scalar coefficients.

    Common Pitfalls in This Lecture

    • Generated-regressor inference. Two-pass Fama-MacBeth betas are estimated, not observed; naive second-pass standard errors understate uncertainty. Apply the Shanken correction or estimate everything jointly by GMM before claiming significance.
    • Look-ahead in factor construction. Factors built with full-sample sorts or rebalanced with future information contaminate every test asset. Replicate published factor timing conventions exactly, including lagging characteristics.

    Summary¶

    What did we learn? - SDF is Universal: All asset pricing models are special cases of \(P_t = E_t[m_{t+1} x_{t+1}]\). - Consumption Puzzles: The standard consumption-based model (CCAPM) fails because consumption is too smooth. It requires absurdly high risk aversion to fit the data. - Factor Models Work Better: Models like Fama-French work well empirically because they use volatile market factors (returns) as proxies for the SDF, rather than smooth consumption data. - Fama-MacBeth: This two-step procedure is the standard tool for testing whether a risk factor is actually priced in the cross-section.

    What is the key takeaway? Asset pricing is the search for the correct SDF. While theory points to consumption, data points to financial factors. The gap between the two—the "dark matter" of finance—is where active research lies.
