# Foundations — Bibliography

> Pathway in one line: learn to read code as an economic argument, then make it reproducible.

## Reading pathway

1. Wilson et al. (2014) — why scientific code needs engineering habits.
2. McKinney — pandas as the working tool of empirical economics.
3. Abelson & Sussman — computation as a language for ideas.
4. Tukey (1977) — look at data before modeling it.
5. Cormen et al. — the complexity vocabulary used across this curriculum.

## Seminal

- **Knuth (1984).** *Literate Programming.* The Computer Journal. — Programs are
  explanations to humans first; the notebook format this curriculum uses is his
  idea carried by tooling.
- **Tukey (1977).** *Exploratory Data Analysis.* Addison-Wesley. — Plots and
  robust summaries before models; the ancestor of every EDA cell below.
- **Abelson & Sussman (1985).** *Structure and Interpretation of Computer
  Programs.* MIT Press. — Abstraction, higher-order functions, and state; the
  deep ideas behind Modules 10–11.

## Modern

- **Wilson et al. (2014).** *Best Practices for Scientific Computing.* PLoS
  Biology. — The reproducibility and style rules the pre-commit hooks enforce.
- **McKinney (2017).** *Python for Data Analysis*, 2nd ed. O'Reilly. — pandas
  from its creator; the direct reference for the data-wrangling lectures.

## Theoretical

- **Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest & Stein (2009).** *Introduction to Algorithms*,
  3rd ed. MIT Press. — The $O(\cdot)$ vocabulary of the complexity lectures
  (22A/22B) and the hash-table mechanics behind `dict`.
